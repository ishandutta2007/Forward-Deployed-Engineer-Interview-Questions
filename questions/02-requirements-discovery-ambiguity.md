# 2. Requirements Discovery & Ambiguity

Figuring out the right problem before building the right solution — arguably the single most distinctive skill this role requires.

---

### 2.1 🟢 What is the difference between a customer's "stated requirement" and their actual underlying "need," and why does an effective Forward Deployed Engineer need to consistently probe beyond the former to identify the latter?

A stated requirement is what a customer explicitly asks for — a specific feature, a specific report format, a specific integration — while the underlying need is the actual operational problem or goal that stated requirement is intended to serve, which may or may not be best addressed by the specific solution the customer initially proposed. An effective Forward Deployed Engineer needs to consistently probe beyond the stated requirement to identify the actual underlying need because customers, quite reasonably, often frame their requests in terms of a specific solution they can imagine (frequently based on how they've solved a similar problem before, or on what they've seen elsewhere), rather than in terms of the actual underlying problem itself — and a stated requirement, taken at face value and implemented literally, sometimes turns out to be a genuinely suboptimal or even actively counterproductive way of addressing the real underlying need once that need is more fully understood, meaning building exactly what was asked for isn't automatically the same as actually solving the customer's real problem, and an FDE who doesn't develop the habit of probing this distinction risks becoming merely an efficient order-taker rather than someone who's actually adding the kind of genuine problem-solving value the role is meant to provide.

---

### 2.2 🟡 What is the "five whys" technique, and how might a Forward Deployed Engineer apply this kind of structured root-cause questioning during an initial discovery conversation with a customer?

The five whys technique involves repeatedly asking "why" in response to a stated problem or requirement, typically several times in succession, to progressively drill down from a surface-level symptom or stated request toward the actual underlying root cause or need driving it. A Forward Deployed Engineer might apply this during an initial discovery conversation by, upon hearing a customer's specific feature request, asking why that specific feature would help, and then asking why that underlying benefit actually matters to their broader operational goals, continuing this iterative questioning until reaching a genuinely fundamental understanding of what problem the customer is actually trying to solve and why it matters to them — this isn't meant to be applied mechanically or in a way that feels interrogative or dismissive of the customer's own expertise and judgment, but as a genuine, curious inquiry technique that helps surface the kind of underlying need discussed in category 2.1, which the customer themselves may not have fully or explicitly articulated even to their own satisfaction, since people quite often haven't consciously worked through the full causal chain connecting their immediate request to their actual, deeper operational goals.

---

### 2.3 🟢 What is the concept of "shadowing" a customer's existing workflow as a specific discovery technique, and why does directly observing how work actually happens often reveal information that a customer wouldn't think to mention in a structured interview or requirements-gathering conversation?

Shadowing involves a Forward Deployed Engineer directly observing a customer's staff actually performing their real, day-to-day work using their existing tools and processes, rather than relying solely on the customer describing that work verbally in a structured interview or requirements-gathering session. Direct observation often reveals information a customer wouldn't think to mention in a purely verbal discussion because much of how experienced people actually do their work has become so habitual and automatic to them that they've stopped consciously noticing or articulating it — the specific manual workarounds they've developed for a known limitation in their existing tools, the informal judgment calls they make that aren't written down anywhere, or the specific moments of friction and frustration they've simply learned to work around and no longer think to mention as a "problem" worth raising — a Forward Deployed Engineer who relies purely on structured interviews without this kind of direct observational component risks missing exactly this category of tacit, unarticulated but often highly consequential operational reality that directly observing real work in action is specifically well-suited to surfacing.

---

### 2.4 🟡 What is the concept of a customer stakeholder's own position and incentives shaping what they emphasize as important, and why does a Forward Deployed Engineer typically need to gather input from multiple different stakeholders at different organizational levels rather than relying on a single point of contact's account of the problem?

Different stakeholders within a customer organization typically have genuinely different vantage points, incentives, and areas of visibility into a given operational problem — a frontline operational staff member who directly experiences a workflow's day-to-day friction often has very different priorities and concerns than a manager focused on team-level performance metrics, who in turn often has different priorities than an executive sponsor focused on the initiative's broader strategic and budgetary justification — and each of these stakeholders' accounts of "the problem" and what would actually constitute a successful solution can reasonably differ in genuine, important ways, none of which is necessarily wrong, but each of which is genuinely partial. A Forward Deployed Engineer typically needs to gather input from multiple different stakeholders at different organizational levels specifically because relying on a single point of contact's account, however well-intentioned and knowledgeable that individual is, risks building a solution that's optimized for that one specific stakeholder's particular vantage point and incentives while missing other genuinely important considerations that only become visible by engaging more broadly across the organization — this multi-stakeholder discovery approach also helps an FDE build the kind of broader organizational buy-in and support that's often genuinely important for a solution's eventual successful adoption, discussed further in category 9.

---

### 2.5 🟢 What is the concept of a discovery or "kickoff" workshop at the start of a forward-deployed engagement, and what specific goals does this kind of structured initial session typically aim to accomplish beyond simply gathering a list of requirements?

A discovery or kickoff workshop is a structured initial working session, typically involving both the Forward Deployed Engineer and multiple relevant customer stakeholders together, held at the outset of an engagement specifically to begin the discovery process discussed throughout this category in a focused, collaborative format. Beyond simply gathering a list of requirements, this kind of session typically aims to accomplish several additional goals — establishing shared, explicit alignment among the different customer stakeholders themselves about what the engagement is actually trying to achieve (which, per the multi-stakeholder discussion in category 2.4, isn't always something the customer's own team has fully and explicitly aligned on internally before the engagement even begins), beginning to build the direct working relationship and trust discussed in category 1.7, and establishing a shared, explicit understanding of the engagement's scope, timeline, and success criteria that all parties genuinely agree to, rather than each party privately carrying their own somewhat different assumptions about what the engagement is actually meant to deliver — a Forward Deployed Engineer should approach this kind of session as a deliberately structured, high-value opportunity to accomplish these broader alignment goals, not merely as an efficient way to collect a requirements list.

---

### 2.6 🟡 What is the concept of "scope creep" specifically as it manifests in forward-deployed engagements, and why might scope creep in this context be a particularly persistent challenge given the close, ongoing, and often informal nature of the customer relationship?

Scope creep refers to a project's actual scope gradually and often informally expanding beyond its originally intended or agreed-upon boundaries, typically through the accumulation of many individually small, seemingly reasonable additional requests rather than through any single, large, formally negotiated expansion. This can be a particularly persistent challenge in forward-deployed engagements specifically because the close, direct, and often informal working relationship with the customer that's genuinely valuable for the discovery process discussed throughout this category also creates natural, frequent opportunities for a customer stakeholder to casually mention "it would also be great if..." requests in the course of day-to-day interaction, and the close, trust-based relationship can make it genuinely harder, both practically and interpersonally, for an FDE to push back on these incremental requests compared to a more formally structured, less personally close engagement model with more explicit change-control processes built in — a Forward Deployed Engineer needs to develop genuine skill in recognizing and appropriately managing this dynamic (discussed further in category 9), neither becoming rigidly inflexible in a way that undermines the relationship and responsiveness that makes the forward-deployed model valuable in the first place, nor allowing scope to expand so freely that the engagement loses focus and fails to actually deliver its core intended value within a reasonable timeframe.

---

### 2.7 🟢 What is the concept of a Minimum Viable Product (MVP) as specifically applied within a forward-deployed engagement, and how does this concept's application in this specific context differ somewhat from its more general, familiar usage in product development?

A Minimum Viable Product is the smallest, most minimal version of a solution that still delivers genuine, meaningful value and allows for real learning about whether the broader approach is actually working, a concept familiar from general product development practice. Applied within a forward-deployed engagement specifically, this concept takes on a somewhat more concrete and immediate character than its more general product-development usage — rather than an MVP being primarily a vehicle for learning what a broad, hypothetical market of future users might want (the more typical general product-development framing), a forward-deployed MVP is typically built and validated against one specific, known customer's actual, concrete workflow and real data, meaning the "learning" an FDE gains from an MVP is generally more direct, specific, and immediately actionable — quickly revealing whether the proposed approach actually works against this specific customer's real operational complexity, rather than requiring the more indirect, aggregated market feedback interpretation that a more general product MVP's evaluation often requires.

---

### 2.8 🟡 What is the concept of "premature solutioning," and why might a Forward Deployed Engineer need to actively resist the urge to propose a specific technical solution too early in an engagement, even when a plausible-seeming solution seems obvious?

Premature solutioning refers to committing to and beginning to build toward a specific technical solution before the underlying problem has actually been sufficiently and rigorously understood, driven by the natural tendency (particularly strong among experienced, capable engineers) to want to move quickly toward building something once a plausible-seeming solution comes to mind. A Forward Deployed Engineer needs to actively resist this urge, even when a plausible solution seems obvious, because an apparently obvious solution based on an initial, still-incomplete understanding of the customer's problem can turn out to be genuinely wrong or suboptimal once the fuller discovery process discussed throughout this category actually surfaces the complete picture — and having already begun building toward a specific technical approach before this fuller understanding is reached creates a real, practical risk of sunk-cost bias making it harder to genuinely reconsider and pivot away from that initial approach even once evidence suggesting it's not the right solution begins to emerge, meaning disciplined, patient adherence to genuine discovery before committing to a specific solution direction is a genuinely important professional discipline this role requires, even though it can feel like it's working against the "move fast" instinct also core to effective forward-deployed practice, discussed in category 1.6.

---

### 2.9 🟢 What is the concept of validating an understanding of the problem through an explicit "read-back" or summary confirmation with the customer, and why is this step considered valuable even when an engineer feels reasonably confident they've correctly understood the customer's needs?

A read-back or summary confirmation involves the Forward Deployed Engineer explicitly articulating back to the customer, in their own words, their current understanding of the customer's problem, priorities, and goals, and specifically inviting the customer to confirm, correct, or refine that understanding before proceeding further. This step is considered valuable even when an engineer feels reasonably confident in their own understanding because it provides a genuine, low-cost opportunity to catch a misunderstanding before it propagates into wasted development effort — miscommunication and misunderstanding are genuinely common even in careful, well-intentioned discovery conversations, particularly given the domain-expertise and terminology gaps that can exist between an engineer and a customer's subject-matter experts, and this kind of explicit confirmation step provides a structured, low-friction opportunity to surface and correct any such misunderstanding while it's still cheap and easy to do so, rather than only discovering a fundamental misunderstanding once a partially- or fully-built solution turns out not to actually match what the customer needed.

### 2.10 🟡 What is the concept of "you can't fully specify what you haven't yet seen work," and why does this principle suggest that some genuine requirements discovery in forward-deployed work can only happen through iterative building and demonstration, rather than through upfront discussion alone, however thorough?

This principle reflects the genuine, well-documented reality that people often struggle to fully and accurately articulate what they actually want or need from a software solution in the abstract, before they've had the opportunity to interact with and react to some concrete, working version of it — a customer might sincerely believe a particular feature or approach is exactly what they need when discussing it in the abstract, only to discover, once they actually see and interact with a working version, that it doesn't quite match their real needs in some specific way they hadn't anticipated or couldn't have articulated in advance. This suggests that some genuine requirements discovery in forward-deployed work can only really happen through this kind of iterative building-and-reacting cycle rather than through upfront discussion alone, however thorough and well-conducted — a Forward Deployed Engineer should build this expectation directly into their working approach, planning for and embracing iterative, frequent demonstration of working (even if still rough or partial) software specifically as a discovery tool in its own right, rather than treating upfront discovery conversation as a phase that should ideally reach full completion and closure before any actual building begins.

---

### 2.11 🟢 What is the concept of a "day in the life" exercise as a specific discovery technique, and how does this technique differ in its specific focus from the general customer interview and shadowing techniques already discussed in this category?

A "day in the life" exercise specifically asks a customer stakeholder (or the Forward Deployed Engineer, having observed enough to attempt this themselves) to walk through, in detailed, chronological narrative form, exactly what a representative day or work session actually involves for the specific role or process being examined — capturing not just the individual tasks involved, but their sequence, their timing, the transitions between them, and how the specific problem being addressed actually fits into this broader, holistic daily context. This differs in its specific focus from more general customer interviews (which might ask about a specific problem or process in relative isolation) and from shadowing (which observes a specific, often narrower slice of actual work in real time) by specifically emphasizing the full, connected, chronological context surrounding the problem being addressed — this broader contextual view can reveal important considerations that a narrower, more targeted discovery approach might miss, including how a proposed solution would actually need to fit into and interact with the surrounding workflow and competing time pressures a user genuinely faces throughout their broader working day, not just the specific narrow task the solution is directly targeting.

---

### 2.12 🟡 What is the concept of distinguishing a customer's "must-have" requirements from their "nice-to-have" requirements, and why is this prioritization exercise often genuinely difficult to conduct accurately, given customers' natural tendency to describe many requests with similar apparent urgency?

Distinguishing must-have from nice-to-have requirements means working to establish a genuine, accurate prioritization among a customer's various stated needs and requests, rather than treating them all as equally critical and urgent. This is often genuinely difficult to conduct accurately because customers, quite naturally and often without fully realizing it, tend to describe many of their various requests with fairly similar apparent urgency and importance, particularly early in an engagement before they've had to make any real, concrete tradeoffs between competing priorities — a Forward Deployed Engineer needs specific, deliberate techniques to help surface a more genuine, accurate prioritization, such as directly and explicitly asking a customer to imagine a scenario where only a subset of their stated requirements could actually be delivered and asking them to identify which specific ones they'd insist on versus which they could live without, or observing which specific issues a customer's own team actually invests the most time and effort working around in their current, existing process (which often reveals a more genuine, revealed prioritization than what people articulate when simply asked to rank their stated preferences in the abstract) — this kind of deliberate, technique-driven prioritization work is a genuinely important skill, since building against an inaccurate understanding of true priority can result in an engagement's early effort being spent on lower-value work while genuinely critical needs remain unaddressed.

---

### 2.13 🟢 What is the concept of a customer's "current state" versus "future state" process mapping, and why is documenting the current state, however imperfect and inefficient it may be, an important discovery step even when the ultimate goal is to help the customer move well beyond it?

Current state process mapping documents how a customer's relevant workflow or process actually functions today, including its genuine inefficiencies, workarounds, and pain points, while future state process mapping describes the envisioned improved process a proposed solution is intended to enable. Documenting the current state, even when it's genuinely inefficient and the ultimate goal is to move well beyond it, is an important discovery step because a clear, accurate understanding of the current state provides essential grounding for designing a genuinely appropriate and successful future state — without this grounding, a proposed future state solution risks either failing to actually address the current state's genuine, specific pain points (having been designed against an idealized or generic conception of the process rather than its actual, messy reality), or failing to account for genuine constraints and dependencies embedded in the current process that any viable future state solution will still need to accommodate or explicitly and deliberately address — a Forward Deployed Engineer should resist the temptation to skip directly to envisioning an ideal future state without first doing this grounding work, even when the current state process seems clearly and obviously flawed.

---

### 2.14 🟢 What is the concept of "you don't know what you don't know" as a specific risk in early-stage discovery, and why might a Forward Deployed Engineer deliberately build in checkpoints specifically designed to surface unknown unknowns rather than only tracking progress against already-identified requirements?

"You don't know what you don't know" captures the genuine risk that some of the most important considerations relevant to a customer's actual problem may not be identified at all during even a careful, deliberate discovery process, simply because no one involved — neither the customer nor the Forward Deployed Engineer — yet has enough visibility or awareness to recognize that a particular consideration is actually relevant and important. A Forward Deployed Engineer might deliberately build in checkpoints specifically designed to surface this kind of unknown unknown — rather than only tracking progress against an already-identified requirements list — by periodically and explicitly asking broader, more open-ended questions like "what haven't we discussed yet that might matter here," or by deliberately seeking out and engaging with additional stakeholders or workflow areas beyond the initially identified scope, specifically to actively probe for considerations that haven't yet surfaced through the discovery process so far, rather than assuming that reaching apparent completeness against an already-identified list of questions and requirements means the discovery process has genuinely covered everything that actually matters.

---

### 2.15 🟢 What is the concept of "requirements drift" as a distinct phenomenon from the scope creep discussed in category 2.6, and why might a Forward Deployed Engineer need a different kind of process discipline to manage each?

Requirements drift refers to a customer's actual, genuine needs and priorities changing over the course of an engagement — not through the accumulation of new, additional requests (the scope creep pattern discussed in category 2.6), but through a genuine evolution or shift in what the customer actually needs or prioritizes, sometimes because their own broader business context or strategic priorities have themselves changed during the engagement, or because their understanding of their own problem has evolved as the engagement has progressed. This requires a somewhat different kind of process discipline than managing scope creep, because while scope creep is generally best managed through deliberate prioritization and change-control discipline (discussed in category 2.6, essentially holding a line against unbounded expansion), requirements drift often reflects a genuine, legitimate change in what actually matters, meaning the appropriate response isn't necessarily to hold firm against the change but to genuinely and periodically re-validate whether the engagement's current direction still matches the customer's actual, current needs — a Forward Deployed Engineer should build in periodic, deliberate check-ins specifically designed to catch this kind of genuine requirements drift (distinct from managing incremental scope creep requests as they arise), recognizing that an engagement that rigidly holds to its originally-defined scope despite a genuine, legitimate shift in the customer's actual needs can end up successfully delivering something the customer no longer actually needs by the time it's complete.

### 2.16 🔴 Three weeks into a forward-deployed engagement, you realize the customer's originally-stated problem was actually a symptom of a different, more fundamental issue in an adjacent part of their organization that wasn't part of the engagement's original scope. How would you approach this situation?

I'd approach this by first validating my own understanding rigorously before raising it with the customer, since a claim this significant — that the actual problem sits somewhere different than originally scoped — deserves real confidence before being presented, echoing the read-back and validation discipline discussed in category 2.9. I'd want to specifically trace and be able to clearly articulate the causal chain connecting the adjacent organizational issue to the originally-stated problem, ideally with specific, concrete evidence gathered through the kind of direct observation and multi-stakeholder discovery discussed throughout this category, rather than presenting this as a vague hunch or intuition. Once I had this well-grounded, I'd want to raise it directly and transparently with the customer's key stakeholders, framing it not as a critique of how the engagement was originally scoped (since the original scoping was presumably done in good faith with the information available at the time) but as a genuine, important discovery that emerged specifically because of the deeper engagement the forward-deployed working model enables — I'd present the specific evidence and reasoning clearly, and I'd want to give the customer stakeholders genuine space to react, ask questions, and potentially push back, rather than presenting this as a fait accompli they simply need to accept. I'd then want to work collaboratively with the customer to figure out the appropriate path forward, which could reasonably range from formally expanding the engagement's scope to address the newly-identified adjacent issue (if the customer agrees this is genuinely important and has the organizational appetite and authority to expand scope this way), to completing the originally-scoped work as planned while clearly documenting and separately flagging the adjacent issue as a distinct, future opportunity the customer can decide whether and when to pursue separately, to, in some cases, a more fundamental conversation about whether the originally-scoped engagement still makes sense at all given this new understanding. Throughout, I'd want to be mindful of the organizational and political reality that the adjacent issue may sit within a different part of the customer's organization with its own stakeholders, ownership, and sensitivities that I likely don't yet have visibility into or relationship with — meaning I'd want to be appropriately careful and collaborative, working through my existing customer relationships to appropriately navigate this rather than unilaterally reaching out to or making claims about a different part of the organization I don't yet have established credibility or context with, recognizing that how this specific finding is communicated and handled organizationally can matter as much as the technical accuracy of the finding itself for the engagement's ultimate success and the broader customer relationship.

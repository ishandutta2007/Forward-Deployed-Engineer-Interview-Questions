# 9. Stakeholder Management & Customer Relationships

The interpersonal and organizational skill this role depends on just as much as raw technical capability.

---

### 9.1 🟢 What is the concept of an "executive sponsor" within a customer organization, and why does the strength and engagement level of this specific relationship often matter disproportionately to a forward-deployed engagement's overall success, beyond the FDE's own day-to-day working relationships?

An executive sponsor is a senior leader within a customer organization who has championed and is genuinely invested in an engagement's success, typically holding enough organizational authority and influence to help secure necessary resources, resolve cross-organizational obstacles, and maintain broader organizational support for the engagement even when it encounters challenges. This relationship often matters disproportionately to an engagement's overall success beyond the FDE's own day-to-day working relationships because a Forward Deployed Engineer's direct, daily working relationships typically exist primarily with more operational, day-to-day stakeholders who often lack the organizational authority to resolve certain kinds of obstacles (a needed resource that a different department controls, an organizational or political obstacle to broader adoption) that only someone with genuine executive-level standing can effectively address — a Forward Deployed Engineer should proactively work to identify who this executive sponsor actually is (or, if no one currently occupies this role, should consider whether cultivating this kind of sponsorship is worth pursuing), and should maintain at least periodic, appropriate visibility and engagement with this relationship, even though the FDE's own most direct, frequent working relationships will typically remain with more operational stakeholders.

### 9.2 🟡 What is the concept of managing "stakeholder alignment" when different customer stakeholders hold genuinely conflicting priorities or visions for what an engagement should actually deliver, and what specific techniques might a Forward Deployed Engineer use to help surface and resolve this kind of conflict constructively?

Managing stakeholder alignment means actively working to identify and, where possible, resolve genuine disagreement or divergence among different customer stakeholders about what an engagement should actually prioritize or deliver, rather than either ignoring this kind of conflict (proceeding as though genuine alignment already exists when it doesn't) or allowing the Forward Deployed Engineer to be caught passively in the middle of stakeholders' unresolved internal disagreements. Specific techniques an FDE might use to help surface and resolve this kind of conflict constructively include explicitly and directly naming the apparent divergence when it becomes visible (rather than avoiding an uncomfortable but genuinely important conversation), facilitating a structured discussion specifically designed to help the different stakeholders understand and work through each other's perspectives and priorities (sometimes bringing a genuinely useful outside, relatively neutral technical perspective to a disagreement that may have become more personally or politically charged among the internal stakeholders themselves), and, where a resolution genuinely can't be reached through this kind of facilitated discussion, escalating the specific, well-characterized disagreement to the executive sponsor discussed in category 9.1 for a final, authoritative decision, rather than the FDE attempting to unilaterally resolve a genuine internal customer organizational disagreement on their own — an FDE should recognize this kind of stakeholder alignment work as a genuinely important, active part of the role, not an unwelcome distraction from the "real" technical work.

### 9.3 🟢 What is the concept of setting explicit, shared expectations about an engagement's scope, timeline, and success criteria early and in writing, and why does this practice help prevent the kind of misaligned expectations that can otherwise damage a customer relationship even when the underlying technical work is genuinely successful?

Setting explicit, shared, written expectations means proactively documenting and confirming, ideally early in an engagement, a clear and specific mutual understanding of what the engagement is actually intended to deliver, by when, and what specific criteria will be used to judge whether it's succeeded — rather than allowing these important parameters to remain implicit, informally discussed, or, worse, understood differently by different parties without this ever being explicitly surfaced and reconciled. This practice helps prevent misaligned expectations from damaging a customer relationship, even when the underlying technical work is genuinely successful, because a customer's actual satisfaction with an engagement often depends less on whether the delivered work is objectively good in some abstract sense, and more on whether it matches what they were actually expecting and hoping for — an engagement that delivers genuinely strong, well-executed technical work can still be perceived as disappointing if it doesn't match a customer's own expectations, which may have been shaped by an overly optimistic initial impression, a miscommunication, or simply differing assumptions that were never explicitly discussed and reconciled — a Forward Deployed Engineer should proactively invest in this kind of explicit, written expectation-setting specifically because it's a comparatively low-cost, high-value practice for avoiding an entirely preventable category of relationship damage that has nothing to do with the actual quality of the underlying technical work.

### 9.4 🟡 What is the concept of proactively communicating bad news or setbacks to a customer, rather than delaying or softening this communication in the hope that the situation will improve before it needs to be raised, and why does this practice, however uncomfortable in the moment, generally serve a Forward Deployed Engineer's longer-term customer relationship and credibility better than the alternative?

Proactively communicating bad news means directly and promptly informing a customer about a genuine setback, delay, or problem as soon as it becomes clear and well-understood, rather than delaying this communication in the hope that the underlying situation will resolve itself or improve before it actually needs to be raised, which is a genuinely common but generally counterproductive instinct, particularly for someone naturally inclined to want to maintain a positive, upbeat relationship with a customer. This practice generally serves a Forward Deployed Engineer's longer-term customer relationship and credibility better than the alternative because a customer who learns about a significant problem or delay only belatedly, especially if they sense it had actually been known for some time before being communicated, tends to lose meaningfully more trust and confidence than a customer who's kept genuinely informed as issues arise, even when the issues themselves are the exact same in both scenarios — proactive, honest communication about a genuine setback, delivered with a clear plan for addressing it, tends to actually build credibility and trust over time (demonstrating exactly the kind of honest, reliable professional practice a customer wants from a trusted technical partner), while delayed or softened communication, even when well-intentioned, tends to erode trust once the customer eventually learns the full, actual situation — a Forward Deployed Engineer should build the habit of this kind of prompt, direct communication even when it's genuinely uncomfortable in the moment, recognizing this discomfort as a reasonable, worthwhile cost relative to the larger relationship and credibility benefit it provides over time.

### 9.5 🟢 What is the concept of a customer's "change fatigue" or organizational capacity for absorbing new tools and workflow changes, and why might a Forward Deployed Engineer need to actively pace an engagement's rollout and adoption expectations around this consideration, rather than assuming a customer can readily absorb as much change as the FDE's own technical delivery pace could otherwise support?

Change fatigue refers to a customer organization's genuine, finite capacity to absorb and adapt to new tools, processes, and workflow changes within a given period, which can become a real, limiting constraint on how much new capability an organization can genuinely and successfully adopt, entirely separate from how quickly a Forward Deployed Engineer might technically be able to build and deliver that new capability. A Forward Deployed Engineer might need to actively pace an engagement's rollout and adoption expectations around this consideration because delivering technically excellent new capability faster than a customer organization can genuinely absorb and successfully integrate into their actual working practices doesn't translate into correspondingly faster genuine value realization — it can instead produce a kind of organizational overwhelm that actually undermines successful adoption, even for capability that's technically sound and would genuinely be valuable if adopted at a more sustainable pace — an FDE should proactively assess and discuss a customer's actual organizational change capacity and appetite (which can vary considerably across different customers and different parts of even the same customer organization) and should be willing to deliberately pace technical delivery and rollout to match this genuine organizational absorption capacity, rather than assuming that delivering as much, as quickly as technically possible is automatically the right approach regardless of the customer's actual capacity to genuinely adopt and benefit from that pace of change.

### 9.6 🟡 What is the concept of identifying and specifically engaging a customer's "resistant" or skeptical stakeholders, rather than focusing primarily on the more enthusiastic, already-supportive stakeholders who are naturally easier and more pleasant to work with?

Identifying and specifically engaging resistant or skeptical stakeholders means proactively seeking out and working to understand the perspective of customer stakeholders who are genuinely hesitant, skeptical, or resistant toward an engagement or its proposed solution, rather than focusing primarily on engaging with the more naturally enthusiastic, already-supportive stakeholders who are typically easier and more immediately pleasant to work with. This is a genuinely important practice because a resistant stakeholder's skepticism, while sometimes uncomfortable to directly engage with, often reflects genuinely important, legitimate concerns or considerations that the more enthusiastic stakeholders may have simply overlooked or under-weighted — a resistant frontline user, for instance, might have specific, well-founded concerns about how a proposed solution would actually affect their day-to-day work that a more senior, less directly hands-on enthusiastic stakeholder genuinely hasn't fully considered — and, separately from the substantive value of understanding their specific concerns, a resistant stakeholder who's never genuinely engaged with and who feels their concerns were never actually heard can become a genuine, sometimes quite effective source of organizational resistance to an engagement's eventual adoption and success, even if they're not among the primary, formal decision-makers — a Forward Deployed Engineer should treat proactively and respectfully engaging with this kind of skeptical stakeholder as a genuinely valuable investment, both for the substantive insight it often provides and for the relationship and adoption benefit of ensuring these stakeholders feel genuinely heard, rather than avoiding this kind of engagement because it feels less immediately comfortable or productive than working with already-enthusiastic supporters.

### 9.7 🟢 What is the concept of a Forward Deployed Engineer needing to "manage up" within their own organization, distinct from managing the customer relationship itself, and what specific information does an FDE typically need to proactively communicate to their own internal leadership?

Managing up within one's own organization means proactively and effectively communicating relevant information, status, risks, and needs to one's own internal leadership and broader organization, a genuinely distinct skill and responsibility from the customer-facing relationship management discussed throughout most of this category. A Forward Deployed Engineer typically needs to proactively communicate several categories of information to their own internal leadership — genuine progress and risk status (giving internal stakeholders an accurate, honest picture of how an engagement is actually going, including genuine challenges and risks, not just a uniformly positive summary), resource and support needs (proactively flagging when additional internal resources, expertise, or organizational support would genuinely help the engagement succeed, rather than silently struggling to manage every challenge entirely independently), and broader strategic insight and learnings (the kind of product insight and generalizable learning discussed in category 1.15, which is often genuinely valuable to the broader organization beyond the specific individual engagement) — an FDE who neglects this internal communication, however strong their direct customer relationship management might be, risks their own organization lacking the visibility needed to provide appropriate support, and risks valuable insight and learning from their engagement never actually reaching and benefiting the broader organization.

### 9.8 🟡 What is the concept of a "trusted advisor" relationship with a customer, as distinct from a purely transactional "vendor" relationship, and what specific behaviors over time tend to differentiate these two genuinely different relationship types?

A trusted advisor relationship is one in which a customer genuinely seeks out and values a Forward Deployed Engineer's perspective and judgment on a broader range of questions and decisions beyond the immediate, narrow scope of a specific technical deliverable, treating the FDE as a genuine, valued partner in thinking through their broader challenges — as distinct from a more purely transactional vendor relationship, in which a customer engages with the FDE specifically and narrowly for the delivery of a specific, defined technical output, without extending this broader trust and consultation to other, adjacent questions and decisions. Specific behaviors that tend to differentiate these two relationship types over time include the FDE consistently demonstrating the kind of genuine domain expertise discussed in category 1.14 (rather than remaining narrowly focused only on the immediate technical task at hand), the FDE consistently demonstrating the kind of honest, proactive communication discussed in category 9.4 (building genuine trust in the FDE's judgment and honesty over time, even and especially when delivering difficult or unwelcome information), and the FDE consistently demonstrating genuine engagement with and investment in the customer's actual, broader success and outcomes (echoing the ownership-of-outcomes discussion in category 1.9), rather than a narrower focus solely on successfully delivering the specific, immediately-scoped technical work — a Forward Deployed Engineer should recognize that earning this kind of trusted advisor relationship, while not always achievable or even appropriate in every single engagement context, generally represents a meaningfully more valuable and durable form of customer relationship than a purely transactional one, both for the customer's own genuine benefit and for the FDE's own broader professional effectiveness and the health of the ongoing customer relationship.

### 9.9 🟢 What is the concept of a customer's "internal politics" genuinely affecting an engagement's actual success or failure, independent of the underlying technical work's objective quality, and why does a Forward Deployed Engineer need at least some working awareness of this organizational dimension even though it's not a purely technical concern?

Internal politics, in this context, refers to the genuine, often unstated organizational dynamics, competing interests, and interpersonal relationships within a customer organization that can meaningfully affect an engagement's actual, real-world success or failure, entirely independent of the underlying technical work's objective quality — an organizationally unpopular or politically disadvantaged internal sponsor's support for an engagement, for instance, can sometimes actively work against that engagement's broader adoption and success regardless of how genuinely excellent the delivered technical solution actually is. A Forward Deployed Engineer needs at least some working awareness of this organizational dimension, even though it's not a purely technical concern within their formal area of expertise, because remaining genuinely oblivious to these dynamics risks an FDE inadvertently taking an action (aligning too closely and visibly with one particular internal faction, for instance, or failing to recognize why a technically sound recommendation is nonetheless meeting unexpected internal resistance) that damages the engagement's actual prospects for reasons that have nothing to do with the underlying technical work's genuine quality — an FDE doesn't need to become a genuine expert in organizational politics or attempt to actively navigate or influence these dynamics in a sophisticated way, but should develop enough situational awareness to recognize when this kind of organizational dynamic is likely at play, and should generally seek guidance from their most trusted, senior customer relationships (or from their own more experienced internal colleagues) when they suspect this kind of dynamic may be affecting an engagement in ways their own more purely technical judgment isn't well-equipped to navigate alone.

### 9.10 🟢 What is the concept of celebrating and explicitly communicating incremental wins throughout a longer engagement, rather than saving all recognition and communication of progress for a single, final delivery milestone, and why might this practice matter particularly for sustaining organizational momentum and support over a longer engagement timeline?

Celebrating and explicitly communicating incremental wins means proactively recognizing and clearly communicating genuine, meaningful progress and value delivered throughout an engagement's ongoing course, rather than saving all such communication and recognition for a single, final delivery milestone at the engagement's eventual conclusion. This practice matters particularly for sustaining organizational momentum and support over a longer engagement timeline because a customer organization's continued enthusiasm, resource allocation, and organizational patience for an ongoing engagement often depends on visible, ongoing evidence that real, tangible progress is actually being made — an engagement that goes for an extended period without this kind of visible, explicitly communicated progress risks a gradual erosion of organizational confidence and support, even if genuine progress is actually occurring beneath the surface, simply because that progress isn't being made sufficiently visible and legible to the broader set of stakeholders whose continued support the engagement depends on — a Forward Deployed Engineer should proactively and deliberately build this kind of regular, explicit progress communication into their working practice throughout an engagement's course, rather than assuming that strong final delivery alone will be sufficient to sustain the organizational momentum and support needed to actually reach that final delivery point in the first place.

### 9.11 🔴 A long-time customer champion who has been your primary point of contact and strongest internal advocate throughout a multi-month engagement is unexpectedly reassigned to a different role within the organization, with a new, previously uninvolved stakeholder taking over as your primary contact. How would you approach this transition to protect the engagement's continuity and momentum?

I'd approach this as a genuine, significant relationship and continuity risk requiring proactive, deliberate management, rather than assuming the engagement's existing momentum and organizational support will automatically transfer smoothly to a new primary contact without this kind of specific, active effort. First, I'd want to move quickly to establish a genuine, direct relationship with the new stakeholder, treating this essentially as launching a compressed version of the discovery and relationship-building process discussed throughout categories 2 and 9 — understanding their own specific background, priorities, and current level of familiarity with the engagement's history, goals, and progress so far, rather than assuming they already share the same context and enthusiasm the departing champion had built up over the full course of the engagement. Second, I'd want to prepare and deliver a genuinely thorough, clear briefing on the engagement's history, current status, and remaining plan specifically for this new stakeholder, treating this as an opportunity to reinforce and clearly communicate the value already delivered (echoing the incremental wins communication discussed in category 9.10) rather than assuming this history and context is something the new stakeholder will simply absorb passively or that doesn't need this kind of deliberate, explicit re-communication. Third, I'd want to proactively assess whether this new stakeholder's own priorities, working style, or level of enthusiasm for the engagement genuinely differ from the departing champion's, since this is a genuine, real possibility that could meaningfully affect how the remainder of the engagement should actually proceed — rather than assuming the new stakeholder will simply continue advocating for and prioritizing the engagement exactly as their predecessor did, I'd want to have a genuine, open conversation to understand their own actual perspective and, if it genuinely differs in some important way from what the engagement has assumed so far, work collaboratively to appropriately adjust rather than proceeding on outdated assumptions. Fourth, I'd want to consider whether this transition also warrants proactively re-engaging the executive sponsor relationship discussed in category 9.1, if one exists, both to ensure continued broader organizational support isn't itself at risk from this specific personnel change, and potentially to seek their help in ensuring the new stakeholder is appropriately positioned and supported to effectively take over this role. And I'd want to communicate this transition and my proactive plan for managing it clearly to my own internal leadership as well, per the managing-up discussion in category 9.7, since a significant customer stakeholder change of this kind represents exactly the sort of genuine engagement risk that my own organization should have visibility into, both to provide any additional support that might genuinely help, and simply because this kind of transparency about real, material engagement risk is itself part of the honest, proactive communication discipline this entire category and repo has emphasized throughout.

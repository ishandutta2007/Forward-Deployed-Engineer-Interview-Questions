# 5. Deployment Environments & Operational Constraints

The real-world infrastructure and environment constraints that shape what's actually buildable and deployable in a given customer's specific context.

---

### 5.1 🟢 What is the concept of an "air-gapped" or fully isolated deployment environment, and what specific engineering constraints does building and deploying software into this kind of environment introduce compared to a standard, internet-connected deployment?

An air-gapped environment is a computing environment that's physically or logically isolated from external networks, including the public internet, typically used in high-security contexts (government, defense, and certain highly regulated industries) where the risk of external network connectivity is considered unacceptable regardless of other security measures in place. Building and deploying software into this kind of environment introduces specific engineering constraints beyond a standard, internet-connected deployment — an application generally cannot rely on any external API calls, cloud services, or internet-based package/dependency downloads during either its build process or its actual runtime operation, meaning all dependencies need to be pre-packaged and brought into the isolated environment through an explicit, controlled transfer process, and any planned functionality that would normally rely on an external service needs to be reconsidered or replaced with an internally-hosted alternative — a Forward Deployed Engineer working in or preparing for this kind of environment needs to plan for this constraint considerably earlier and more deliberately than in a standard deployment context, since discovering a hidden external dependency only once attempting an actual air-gapped deployment can be a costly, hard-to-quickly-remediate surprise.

### 5.2 🟡 What is the concept of "on-premises" (on-prem) deployment as distinct from cloud-hosted deployment, and what specific operational and engineering considerations does a Forward Deployed Engineer need to weigh when a customer requires or strongly prefers on-prem deployment for a given engagement?

On-premises deployment means an application and its underlying infrastructure runs on hardware and infrastructure physically located within and directly controlled by the customer's own facilities and IT organization, rather than on infrastructure hosted and managed by a third-party cloud provider. Specific considerations a Forward Deployed Engineer needs to weigh when a customer requires this deployment model include the customer's own specific, often older or more constrained hardware and infrastructure capabilities (which may not match the flexibility and elasticity a cloud environment more typically provides), the customer's own IT team's specific operational processes and constraints for provisioning and managing infrastructure (which may move considerably slower and involve more approval overhead than the FDE's own team might be accustomed to), and the practical reality that ongoing maintenance, monitoring, and troubleshooting of an on-prem deployment typically requires more direct coordination with and dependency on the customer's own IT staff, rather than the more self-contained operational control a cloud-hosted deployment more typically affords — a Forward Deployed Engineer working on an on-prem engagement needs to build genuine, early, and ongoing collaborative relationships with the customer's IT operations staff specifically to navigate these considerations effectively, treating this relationship as a genuinely important and distinct part of the engagement's success factors, not merely an incidental logistics detail.

---

### 5.3 🟢 What is the concept of a "bring your own cloud" (BYOC) or customer-hosted deployment model, and how does this model differ from both a fully vendor-hosted SaaS deployment and a fully on-premises deployment discussed in category 5.2?

A bring-your-own-cloud deployment model has an application deployed within the customer's own cloud provider account or environment (which the customer controls and pays for directly), rather than within infrastructure directly owned and managed by the software vendor (as in a fully vendor-hosted SaaS model) or within infrastructure physically located at the customer's own facilities (as in a fully on-premises model, discussed in category 5.2). This model offers something of a middle ground between these two other approaches — retaining much of the flexibility and elasticity benefit of modern cloud infrastructure (unlike the more constrained on-premises model) while still keeping the customer's data and infrastructure within an environment they directly control and are billed for, which can be an important consideration for customers with strict data residency, security, or compliance requirements that make a fully vendor-hosted model unacceptable but that don't require the full isolation of an on-premises deployment — a Forward Deployed Engineer working within this model needs to understand that they're typically deploying and operating within infrastructure they don't have full, unilateral administrative control over, requiring appropriate coordination with the customer's own cloud account administrators for provisioning, permissions, and ongoing infrastructure management, somewhat similar in spirit to the on-prem coordination discussed in category 5.2, even though the underlying infrastructure technology itself is more modern and flexible than a typical on-prem environment.

### 5.4 🟡 What is the concept of a customer's "change management" or "change advisory board" (CAB) process, and why might this kind of formal organizational process, common at larger, more risk-averse enterprise customers, meaningfully affect a Forward Deployed Engineer's actual development and deployment velocity?

A change management or change advisory board process is a formal organizational process, common at larger, more risk-averse enterprise organizations (particularly those in regulated industries or those managing genuinely mission-critical operational systems), requiring any proposed change to a production system or environment to go through a structured review and approval process before it can actually be deployed, often involving a scheduled review meeting, formal documentation of the proposed change and its risk assessment, and a defined approval timeline that may not align with a Forward Deployed Engineer's own preferred, faster iteration pace. This kind of formal process can meaningfully affect an FDE's actual development and deployment velocity because even a small, low-risk change that could technically be deployed within minutes from a pure engineering perspective may need to wait for the customer's next scheduled change advisory board review, which might occur only weekly or even less frequently, and might require submitting formal documentation well in advance of that scheduled review — a Forward Deployed Engineer working with a customer that has this kind of formal process needs to actively and proactively plan their development and deployment cadence around it (batching and preparing changes with sufficient lead time to meet the customer's actual review and approval cadence) rather than assuming they can maintain the same fast, unconstrained iteration pace that might be achievable with a smaller, less formally structured customer, and should factor this constraint explicitly into engagement timeline expectations set with both the customer and their own internal stakeholders.

### 5.5 🟢 What is the concept of a customer's existing infrastructure and technology constraints (a specific, older database version, a specific legacy operating system, a specific required programming language or framework) shaping what a Forward Deployed Engineer can actually build, and why does discovering these constraints early in an engagement matter considerably more than discovering them later?

A customer's existing infrastructure often comes with specific, sometimes quite restrictive technical constraints — a required compatibility with an older database or operating system version, an internal policy mandating use of a specific programming language or technology stack, or specific network or security configuration limitations — that meaningfully shape what technical approaches are actually viable for a given engagement, regardless of what a Forward Deployed Engineer might otherwise consider technically ideal or most familiar. Discovering these constraints early in an engagement matters considerably more than discovering them later because a technical approach or architecture chosen without awareness of a significant, binding constraint can require substantial, costly rework once that constraint is eventually discovered, particularly if meaningful development has already proceeded based on an incompatible assumption — a Forward Deployed Engineer should make investigating and confirming a customer's actual, specific technical environment constraints (not just their stated general preferences, which can sometimes be incomplete or not fully account for constraints the customer's own staff may not think to proactively mention) an explicit, early priority within the broader discovery process discussed throughout category 2, rather than treating this kind of technical environment investigation as a separate, lower-priority concern to be addressed only once the "real" discovery work is complete.

### 5.6 🟡 What is the concept of a customer's network security posture (firewall rules, VPN requirements, network segmentation) creating genuine friction for a Forward Deployed Engineer's own ability to actually access and work within a customer's environment, and how might an FDE proactively manage this friction rather than being repeatedly surprised by it?

A customer's network security posture — including firewall rules restricting what network traffic is permitted, VPN requirements for accessing internal systems, and network segmentation isolating different parts of the customer's own internal infrastructure from each other — can create genuine, sometimes substantial friction for a Forward Deployed Engineer's own practical ability to access and work within the customer's environment, entirely separate from any friction related to the actual engineering work itself. An FDE can proactively manage this friction, rather than being repeatedly surprised by it throughout an engagement, by treating early, explicit investigation of exactly what access and connectivity will actually be needed (and what the customer's specific process and typical timeline for granting that access actually is) as a genuinely important early discovery and planning task, ideally raised and initiated well before it becomes an active blocker to planned work — since access provisioning processes at larger, more security-conscious organizations can themselves take meaningfully longer than an FDE's own preferred development pace might assume, and repeatedly discovering a new, unanticipated access barrier partway through active development work (needing access to yet another specific internal system or network segment that wasn't anticipated or requested during an earlier, single upfront access request) can introduce real, avoidable delay that proactive, comprehensive upfront planning specifically helps minimize.

### 5.7 🟢 What is the concept of a customer's data residency or data sovereignty requirements, and why might these requirements meaningfully constrain where and how a Forward Deployed Engineer can actually store, process, or even transmit a customer's data during an engagement?

Data residency or data sovereignty requirements refer to legal, regulatory, or internal policy requirements specifying that certain categories of data must be stored, and in some cases processed, within a specific geographic jurisdiction, rather than being freely transferred to or processed within infrastructure located elsewhere, echoing the data residency discussion found in adjacent AI clinical data management literature. These requirements can meaningfully constrain a Forward Deployed Engineer's actual technical options during an engagement — a cloud infrastructure choice, a specific third-party service or API the FDE might otherwise want to use, or even simply where the FDE's own development and testing environment can be physically located, may all be directly constrained by a customer's applicable data residency requirements — a Forward Deployed Engineer working with a customer subject to meaningful data residency constraints needs to understand these requirements specifically and concretely early in an engagement (rather than assuming they can freely use whatever infrastructure or tooling they'd normally default to), and should factor this understanding directly into their technical planning and architecture decisions from the outset, since discovering a fundamental incompatibility between a chosen technical approach and the customer's binding data residency requirements only after meaningful development work has already proceeded represents exactly the kind of costly, avoidable rework this kind of early, deliberate investigation is specifically meant to prevent.

### 5.8 🟡 What is the concept of building and testing software against a customer's actual production-representative data volume and complexity, and why might a solution that works perfectly well against a smaller sample dataset during development fail or perform unacceptably once deployed against the customer's full, real-world data scale?

Building and testing against production-representative data volume means validating a solution's actual behavior, correctness, and performance using data that genuinely reflects the scale, volume, and real-world complexity the solution will actually need to handle in full production use, rather than only validating against a smaller, more convenient sample dataset used during initial, faster-paced development. A solution that works perfectly well against a smaller sample can fail or perform unacceptably once deployed against a customer's full, real-world data scale for several genuinely common reasons — certain data quality issues or edge cases (per the discussion throughout category 3) may simply not be present in a smaller, cleaner sample dataset but become genuinely common and impactful at full production scale, and certain algorithms or data processing approaches that perform acceptably against a small dataset can show meaningfully different, sometimes drastically worse, performance characteristics once applied to data at genuinely production scale, due to how their underlying computational complexity actually scales — a Forward Deployed Engineer should build in explicit validation against production-representative data volume and complexity as a deliberate, planned step well before a solution is expected to actually operate at full production scale, rather than discovering scale-related problems only once a full production deployment has already begun and issues start manifesting in a live, customer-facing context.

### 5.9 🟢 What is the concept of a customer's existing monitoring, logging, and alerting infrastructure, and why might a Forward Deployed Engineer need to integrate with or work around this existing infrastructure rather than simply introducing their own preferred, separate observability tooling?

A customer's existing monitoring, logging, and alerting infrastructure refers to whatever tools and systems the customer's own IT and operations teams already use to monitor and maintain visibility into their broader technical environment, which a Forward Deployed Engineer's newly-built application typically needs to integrate with in some meaningful way, rather than existing as an entirely separate, isolated island of observability the customer's own operations staff aren't familiar with or don't have visibility into. A Forward Deployed Engineer might need to integrate with or work around this existing infrastructure, rather than simply introducing their own separately preferred observability tooling, because the customer's own operations team is often ultimately responsible for the ongoing operational health of systems within their environment (particularly for a solution intended to persist and be operated by the customer's own staff beyond the initial engagement, echoing the discussion throughout category 7), and a solution whose operational health and behavior isn't visible through the tools and processes that team already knows and relies on creates a genuine, practical operational gap that can become a real problem once the FDE's own direct, hands-on involvement in the engagement eventually winds down — a Forward Deployed Engineer should proactively investigate what monitoring and observability infrastructure a customer's operations team already relies on and should generally favor integrating with it, even if this requires somewhat more upfront integration effort than simply deploying a separate, unfamiliar tool the FDE personally prefers.

### 5.10 🟢 What is the concept of "sandboxed" or non-production environments provided by enterprise customers for development and testing purposes, and what specific limitation should a Forward Deployed Engineer be aware of regarding how faithfully a sandbox environment actually represents the customer's real production environment?

A sandboxed or non-production environment is a separate, typically lower-stakes environment a customer provides specifically for development, testing, and experimentation purposes, allowing a Forward Deployed Engineer to build and validate work without the risk of directly affecting the customer's actual, live production systems and operations. A specific limitation to be aware of is that a sandbox environment, however genuinely useful and important for safe development, isn't always a fully faithful, complete representation of the customer's real production environment — it may run on a different, smaller-scale infrastructure configuration, may contain synthetic or otherwise non-representative test data rather than the customer's genuine, full-scale production data (directly relevant to the production-representative data testing discussion in category 5.8), and may lack certain integrations, configurations, or access restrictions that are actually present in the real production environment — a Forward Deployed Engineer should treat successful validation within a sandbox environment as a genuinely valuable but not fully sufficient confirmation of a solution's real-world readiness, and should build in an explicit plan for validating the solution's actual behavior against the real production environment's genuine characteristics before considering it fully validated, rather than assuming sandbox success automatically and completely predicts production success.

---

### 5.11 🟡 What is the concept of a customer's existing identity and access management (IAM) system, and why does properly integrating a newly-built application with this existing system (rather than building a separate, standalone authentication mechanism) matter for both security and genuine user adoption?

A customer's existing identity and access management system is the infrastructure they already use to manage user authentication and authorization across their broader technical environment (commonly a system supporting single sign-on, allowing users to authenticate once and gain appropriate access across multiple different internal applications and systems). Properly integrating a newly-built application with this existing system, rather than building a separate, standalone authentication mechanism specific to the new application alone, matters for security because a separate, standalone authentication system represents an additional, independent attack surface and an additional set of credentials that need to be independently managed and secured, rather than benefiting from whatever centralized security controls, monitoring, and policy enforcement the customer's existing IAM system already provides — and it matters for genuine user adoption because requiring users to remember and manage yet another separate set of login credentials, distinct from the single sign-on experience they're already accustomed to across their other work tools, introduces real, practical friction that can meaningfully suppress actual usage and adoption of an otherwise valuable new application — a Forward Deployed Engineer should generally treat proper integration with a customer's existing identity and access management system as an important, relatively early priority, rather than deferring this integration as a lower-priority concern to be addressed only after the application's "real" core functionality is otherwise complete.

### 5.12 🔴 A customer's IT team informs you, partway through an active engagement, that the environment you've been developing against doesn't actually match their real production environment in several specific ways (different infrastructure scale, different network configuration, some different installed software versions), and this wasn't communicated clearly at the engagement's outset. How would you approach both the immediate technical remediation and the broader relationship and process implications?

I'd approach this as a genuine, two-part problem requiring both immediate technical action and a broader, more reflective conversation about how this miscommunication happened and how to prevent it going forward, rather than treating it purely as a technical issue to be quietly fixed. On the immediate technical side, I'd want to systematically and specifically understand exactly what the actual differences are between what I've been developing against and the real production environment, prioritizing this investigation over continuing to build additional new functionality until I have this clear picture, since continuing to build against a known-inaccurate environment risks compounding the eventual remediation effort — I'd want to specifically assess which of my existing work is likely genuinely unaffected by these specific discrepancies, which may need modest adjustment, and which may need more substantial rework, treating this as a structured risk assessment rather than assuming uniformly minor or uniformly severe impact without this kind of specific investigation. I'd want to prioritize getting direct, hands-on access to validate my work against the actual real production environment characteristics as quickly as reasonably possible, even if this means some temporary disruption to my planned development sequence, since continuing to validate against the known-inaccurate environment provides diminishing, and potentially actively misleading, confidence at this point. On the relationship and process side, I'd want to have a direct, honest, and non-accusatory conversation with the customer's IT team and relevant broader stakeholders about how this discrepancy arose — not to assign blame, but because understanding the root cause (was this a genuine miscommunication, a change that occurred in the production environment after the engagement began without being communicated, or simply an honest oversight in the initial environment setup and description) is genuinely important both for appropriately and fairly assessing the actual impact and required remediation, and for establishing a better, more reliable process going forward to prevent a similar issue recurring later in the same engagement. I'd want to specifically propose and establish a clearer, more explicit process for validating environment parity going forward — perhaps a periodic, structured check-in specifically comparing the development/testing environment's characteristics against the actual production environment, rather than relying on a single upfront description at the engagement's outset that, as this situation demonstrates, may not have been fully complete or accurate, or may have become outdated as the production environment itself continued to evolve during the engagement. And I'd want to communicate the overall situation, its technical impact, and the proposed remediation plan clearly and transparently to my own internal stakeholders and to the broader customer relationship (not just the specific IT team I've been working most directly with), since a discrepancy of this nature and scale is the kind of issue that can reasonably affect the engagement's timeline and that deserves this kind of proactive, transparent communication rather than being handled quietly and only surfacing if and when it eventually causes a more visible, harder-to-explain problem.
